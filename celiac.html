Celiac disease

PRIMARY SOURCE: ACG Clinical Guidelines: Diagnosis and Management of Celiac Disease (Rubio-Tapia et al., 2023)

GI HandbookSmall Bowel, Colon & Diarrhea · Celiac disease→

Bottom line

Test on a gluten-containing diet — the single most common cause of a falsely negative work-up is a patient who already went gluten-free. Start with tTG-IgA plus a total IgA; confirm a positive with duodenal biopsy (≥4 distal samples + 1–2 from the bulb) before committing someone to a lifelong diet. Then treat with a strict gluten-free diet and hunt for the deficiencies celiac leaves behind.

Investigate further
  • Weight loss, worsening diarrhea, or abdominal pain despite a strict gluten-free diet → refractory celiac disease vs enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL) — refer
  • Severe malabsorption with hypoalbuminemia, dehydration, or electrolyte derangement
  • New iron-deficiency anemia or unexplained transaminitis — celiac is a frequently missed cause

Serologic testing (on gluten)

  • tTG-IgA + total IgA is the first-line combination. If IgA-deficient, use IgG-based tests (DGP-IgG or tTG-IgG).
  • Whom to test: chronic diarrhea/malabsorption, iron-deficiency anemia, unexplained transaminitis, osteoporosis at a young age, first-degree relatives, and associated conditions (type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid, Down/Turner syndrome, dermatitis herpetiformis).
  • If the patient is already gluten-free, either reintroduce gluten before testing or use HLA typing to help exclude the diagnosis.

Confirm before you commit

  • Positive serology → EGD with duodenal biopsies: at least four from the distal duodenum plus one to two from the bulb. Histology shows intraepithelial lymphocytosis, crypt hyperplasia, and villous atrophy (Marsh grading).
  • HLA-DQ2/DQ8 has high negative predictive value: a negative result essentially excludes celiac and is most useful for ruling out equivocal cases or patients already on a gluten-free diet.
  • Biopsy remains the standard in adults. ACG 2023 allows a no-biopsy diagnosis only in selected adults who can't or won't have endoscopy: tTG-IgA ≥10× ULN plus a positive EMA on a second sample.

Treat and monitor

  • Strict, lifelong gluten-free diet with dietitian support — the only effective therapy.
  • Screen and replace deficiencies: iron, folate, vitamin B12, vitamin D, and check for metabolic bone disease with DEXA.
  • Monitor response with symptoms and falling tTG-IgA titers; persistent symptoms usually mean ongoing gluten exposure (often inadvertent).
  • Persistent villous atrophy on a genuinely strict diet raises refractory celiac disease and warrants specialist evaluation.
Pearls for the note
  • "Gluten-free for a month, now testing" is the classic error — write whether the patient was eating gluten at the time of testing.
  • Order a total IgA with the tTG; selective IgA deficiency is over-represented in celiac and makes tTG-IgA falsely negative.
  • Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia or transaminitis is a celiac screen, not just a GI-bleed or liver work-up.
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis is celiac of the skin — the diagnosis can be made from skin biopsy plus serology.

StudyYearOne-line verdict
