Acute GI · Bleeding

Lower GI bleeding

PRIMARY SOURCES: ACG Guideline, Hematochezia/LGIB (Sengupta et al., 2023) · HALT-IT Lancet 2020

Bottom line

Roughly 80% of lower GI bleeds stop spontaneously. Your job is to find the ones that won't: hemodynamically significant bleeding goes to CT angiography first, not an urgent colonoscopy through an unprepped colon. For everyone else, colonoscopy on the next available list after an adequate prep — trials show no benefit to scoping within 24 hours. And don't give tranexamic acid.

Escalate now
  • Hemodynamic instability despite resuscitation → CT angiography now; positive CTA → IR embolization
  • Hematochezia with instability: rule out a brisk upper source (10–15% of "lower" bleeds) — consider EGD
  • Ongoing transfusion requirement without a localized source

Triage: is this actually a lower bleed, and is it stable?

  • Restrictive transfusion (Hgb <7; ~8 with cardiovascular disease), two large-bore IVs, type & screen.
  • Brisk hematochezia + hemodynamic compromise: think upper source until excluded — a BUN:Cr ratio >30 or melena history points up.
  • Risk scores (Oakland) can support early discharge of minor, self-limited bleeds.

Unstable or ongoing bleeding → CT angiography first

The ACG 2023 guideline positions CTA as the initial diagnostic study for hemodynamically significant hematochezia: fast, no prep, localizes bleeding at rates as low as ~0.3–0.5 mL/min, and maps the target for IR. A positive CTA should be followed promptly by transcatheter embolization. A negative CTA in a stabilized patient de-escalates you back to elective colonoscopy.

Stable patients: colonoscopy without the 3 a.m. prep

Randomized trials and meta-analyses found no improvement in rebleeding, mortality, or intervention rates with colonoscopy <24 h versus routine timing. Order a real prep (4–6 L split-dose PEG) and put the patient on the next appropriate list. Diverticular bleeding — the most common cause — is treated endoscopically with clips (through-the-scope or over-the-scope) when a culprit is found.

What not to do

  • No tranexamic acid. HALT-IT (Lancet 2020, ~12,000 patients): no mortality benefit, more venous thromboembolism.
  • No routine urgent colonoscopy for stable bleeds, and no unprepped scope — visualization is the whole point.
  • Don't chase a tagged RBC scan first when CTA is available; use it for slow, intermittent, hard-to-localize bleeding.

Antithrombotics around a lower bleed

  • Hold anticoagulation during hemodynamically significant bleeding; resume once hemostasis is achieved — usually within days, because thrombotic risk off therapy dominates.
  • Continue aspirin for secondary cardiovascular prevention; primary-prevention aspirin usually retires here permanently.
  • Reversal agents are for life-threatening bleeding, not for a stable Hgb of 8.9.
Pearls for the consult note
  • "CTA-negative, hemodynamically stable" is an evidence-backed reason to prep tonight and scope tomorrow — cite the timing trials when pushed.
  • Ischemic colitis after hypotension (sepsis, dialysis, cardiac events) is a diagnosis you can often make before the scope.
  • Document the anticoagulation restart plan in your discharge summary — it's the most commonly dropped ball in LGIB.
