Acute GI · Bleeding

Upper GI bleeding (nonvariceal)

PRIMARY SOURCES: ACG Guideline, Upper GI & Ulcer Bleeding (Laine et al., 2021) · Villanueva NEJM 2013 · Lau NEJM 2020

GI HandbookGI Bleeding · Upper GI bleeding→

Bottom line

Resuscitate with a restrictive transfusion threshold (Hgb <7), risk-stratify with Glasgow-Blatchford, start a PPI, and get endoscopy within 24 hours — not within 6. Very-low-risk patients (GBS 0–1) can go home. If cirrhosis is possible, treat as variceal until proven otherwise (see the variceal page).

Call GI now — not in the morning
  • Hemodynamic instability despite resuscitation, or ongoing hematemesis
  • Suspected variceal bleed (known cirrhosis, stigmata of portal hypertension)
  • Bleeding after recent endoscopic intervention or in a post-surgical anatomy
  • Brisk bleed in a patient who cannot be transfused or refuses blood

Resuscitate — restrictively

Two large-bore IVs, crystalloid for hypotension, type & screen. Transfuse RBCs at Hgb <7 g/dL (target 7–9). Villanueva (NEJM 2013) showed restrictive transfusion improved survival versus a liberal threshold of 9 — the counterintuitive result worth quoting on rounds. Use a higher threshold (~8) for patients with acute coronary syndrome or hemodynamic instability where you're chasing losses.

  • Massive bleed: activate MTP, protect the airway before endoscopy if ongoing hematemesis or encephalopathy.
  • Correct coagulopathy pragmatically — do not delay endoscopy for an INR <2.5.

Risk-stratify at the door

The ACG 2021 guideline recommends the Glasgow-Blatchford score: a GBS of 0–1 identifies patients at very low risk of needing transfusion, intervention, or dying — appropriate for outpatient management with early follow-up. Everyone else is admitted; higher scores predict need for intervention.

Medications before the scope

  • PPI: start IV PPI on arrival (e.g., pantoprazole 80 mg IV bolus). Pre-endoscopy PPI downstages lesions but has not been shown to change hard outcomes — give it, but never let it delay endoscopy.
  • Erythromycin 250 mg IV 20–90 min pre-endoscopy: a prokinetic that empties the stomach and improves visualization (ACG 2021 recommends it).
  • Stop what's hurting: hold NSAIDs; anticoagulant decisions are individualized — see the periprocedural page.
  • NG lavage is not required for diagnosis or prognosis.

Endoscopy within 24 hours — urgency has a ceiling

Perform EGD within 24 h of presentation after resuscitation. Lau (NEJM 2020) randomized high-risk patients to endoscopy within 6 h versus 6–24 h and found no mortality benefit to urgent endoscopy — earlier scoping of an under-resuscitated patient helps no one. Findings are graded by Forrest class, which drives therapy and rebleeding risk.

After the scope

  • High-risk stigmata treated endoscopically: high-dose PPI for 72 h — continuous infusion (8 mg/h after 80 mg bolus) or intermittent high-dose IV/oral dosing are both acceptable (ACG 2021), then oral PPI BID for 2 weeks, then daily.
  • Rebleeding: repeat endoscopy first; transcatheter arterial embolization if endoscopic retreatment fails; surgery is last.
  • Test for H. pylori in every ulcer bleed, treat if positive, and confirm eradication. A negative test during acute bleeding should be repeated later — sensitivity drops in the acute setting.
  • Restart anticoagulation once hemostasis is achieved — for most indications the thrombotic risk of staying off exceeds rebleeding risk; resuming warfarin is associated with lower mortality.
  • If NSAIDs are truly unavoidable: lowest dose, COX-2 selective, plus PPI.
Pearls for the consult note
  • A BUN:creatinine ratio >30 suggests an upper source — digested blood is a protein meal.
  • Document GBS with its components; "GBS 0, discharged with 72-h follow-up" is a defensible, guideline-cited plan.
  • Melena can persist for days after hemostasis; a downtrending Hgb requirement matters more than stool color.
  • "Coffee-ground emesis, hemodynamically stable, Hgb stable" rarely needs a 2 a.m. scope — say why: Lau 2020.
