Acute mesenteric ischemia

PRIMARY SOURCES: WSES Guidelines on acute mesenteric ischemia (2017, updated 2022) · ESVS Guidelines (2017) · Clair & Beach NEJM 2016

GI HandbookSmall Bowel, Colon & Diarrhea · Colonic ischemia and acute mesenteric ischemia→

Bottom line

Pain out of proportion to exam plus a risk factor is mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise. The diagnosis is missed because early exams are unremarkable and lactate is a late sign. Move fast: CT angiography immediately, resuscitate, start heparin and broad-spectrum antibiotics, and get surgery and interventional radiology involved before the bowel infarcts. A normal lactate does not exclude it.

This is the emergency
  • Peritonitis, rising lactate, or hemodynamic instability → operating room for exploration/resection, don't wait for more tests
  • Atrial fibrillation or recent MI + sudden severe abdominal pain → arterial embolism to the SMA
  • Any delay is the enemy — mortality climbs sharply once transmural infarction occurs

Recognize the four mechanisms

  • Arterial embolism (≈50%): sudden pain, cardiac source (AF, recent MI, valvular disease).
  • Arterial thrombosis: often on prior atherosclerosis, may have "intestinal angina" (postprandial pain, food fear, weight loss) beforehand.
  • Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI): low-flow state — shock, pressors, dialysis, cardiac disease — with patent vessels but splanchnic vasoconstriction.
  • Mesenteric venous thrombosis: subacute pain, hypercoagulable states, portal hypertension.

Diagnose without delay

  • CT angiography (arterial + venous phases) is the test — do not delay for lactate or an unprepped work-up.
  • Labs support but never exclude: lactate, WBC, and metabolic acidosis are late. Trust the story over reassuring numbers.
  • Plain films and non-contrast CT are inadequate; pneumatosis and portal venous gas are ominous, late findings.

Treat in parallel, not in sequence

  • Immediate resuscitation, systemic anticoagulation (heparin), broad-spectrum antibiotics, and correction of the low-flow state (minimize vasoconstrictors in NOMI).
  • Revascularize: surgical embolectomy/bypass or endovascular therapy for arterial occlusion; anticoagulation is the mainstay for venous thrombosis.
  • Peritonitis or non-viable bowel → laparotomy with resection; a second-look operation is often planned to reassess bowel viability.
  • NOMI: treat the underlying shock; intra-arterial vasodilators (e.g., papaverine) in selected cases.
Pearls for the note
  • "Soft abdomen, severe pain, normal lactate" is the classic dangerous reassurance — this presentation buys a CTA, not observation.
  • New-onset AF with abdominal pain is SMA embolism until imaged.
  • Postprandial pain, food fear, and weight loss is chronic mesenteric ischemia — the warning shot before an acute thrombosis.
  • Portal venous gas or pneumatosis on CT means you are already late; escalate immediately.
