Cholangitis & choledocholithiasis

PRIMARY SOURCES: Tokyo Guidelines 2018 · ASGE Guideline, suspected choledocholithiasis (2019)

GI HandbookPancreas & Biliary · Choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, and biliary strictures→

Bottom line

Cholangitis is sepsis with an anatomic cause: antibiotics plus biliary drainage (ERCP) within 24–48 hours, faster if severe. For suspected common bile duct stones without cholangitis, use the ASGE probability ladder: high probability → ERCP; intermediate → EUS or MRCP first; low → cholecystectomy with intraoperative cholangiogram. Don't send an intermediate-probability patient to ERCP "just to look."

Escalate now
  • Cholangitis with hypotension, altered mentation, or organ dysfunction (Tokyo Grade III) → ICU + urgent decompression (within ~12–24 h)
  • Cholangitis not improving on antibiotics within 24 h → move the ERCP up
  • ERCP not feasible or failed → percutaneous transhepatic drainage or EUS-guided drainage — the bile must come out one way or another

Recognize cholangitis: Charcot is a hint, Tokyo is the standard

Charcot's triad (fever, jaundice, RUQ pain) is specific but insensitive. Tokyo 2018 diagnoses on three axes: (A) systemic inflammation — fever or elevated WBC/CRP, (B) cholestasis — jaundice or elevated LFTs, (C) imaging — biliary dilation or an etiology (stone, stent, stricture). Suspected = A + one of B/C; definite = one from each.

Tokyo severity → urgency of drainage
GradeDefinitionDrainage
I (mild)No organ dysfunction, responds to initial therapyWithin 24–48 h; some resolve and can have semi-elective ERCP
II (moderate)WBC extremes, high fever, age ≥75, bili ≥5, hypoalbuminemiaEarly — within 24–48 h
III (severe)Any organ dysfunction (pressors, altered mentation, P/F <300, AKI, INR, platelets)Urgent, alongside ICU resuscitation

Resuscitate and cover the biliary tree

  • Blood cultures, lactate, aggressive fluids — treat as sepsis.
  • Antibiotics covering Enterobacterales and anaerobes per local antibiogram: piperacillin-tazobactam, or ceftriaxone + metronidazole; carbapenem for prior resistant organisms or healthcare-associated infection. Narrow on cultures; short courses (~4–5 days after source control) suffice for most.

Suspected CBD stones without cholangitis: the ASGE ladder

ASGE 2019 probability stratification
ProbabilityCriteriaNext move
HighCBD stone on imaging, or clinical cholangitis, or total bilirubin >4 mg/dL plus dilated CBDERCP
IntermediateAbnormal LFTs, age >55, dilated CBD alone, or bili 1.8–4EUS or MRCP first; ERCP only if positive
LowNone of the aboveCholecystectomy ± intraoperative cholangiogram

The point of the ladder: ERCP carries real risk (post-ERCP pancreatitis ~5–10%) and should be therapeutic, not exploratory. Rectal indomethacin is standard prophylaxis in high-risk ERCP — you'll see it on the post-procedure orders.

Close the loop: the gallbladder

  • After ERCP for stones or after cholangitis from gallstones: cholecystectomy, ideally same admission — recurrence of biliary events off surgery is high.
  • Non-operative candidates: ERCP with sphincterotomy provides partial protection; document the shared decision.
  • Acute cholecystitis (a different disease — Murphy's sign, wall thickening, normal-ish bilirubin) → early cholecystectomy; percutaneous cholecystostomy for the truly unfit.
Pearls for the consult note
  • Gallstone pancreatitis + cholangitis is the one pancreatitis that needs urgent ERCP — otherwise ERCP doesn't treat pancreatitis.
  • A normal-caliber CBD on ultrasound doesn't exclude stones; LFT trajectory over 24–48 h is often more informative than the first set.
  • When you page GI, have ready: Tokyo grade, INR/platelets, anticoagulants, NPO time, and pressor status — it's the difference between "ERCP today" and phone tag.
  • Rapidly normalizing LFTs suggest a passed stone — that patient may need EUS/MRCP or just cholecystectomy, not ERCP.
